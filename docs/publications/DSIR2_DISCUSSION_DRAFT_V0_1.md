# DSIR-2 Discussion draft v0.1

**Date:** 2026-08-28  
**Status:** publication prose source; not yet integrated into active manuscript.

## Discussion

The central result of DSIR-2 is not that one response channel uniquely identifies a dark-sector mechanism. It is that the meaning of response-space separation depends on the object being compared. A selected parameter displacement is an oriented vector; a locally admissible nuisance deformation may instead be a line or a higher-dimensional subspace. Those objects can yield qualitatively different conclusions even when they are built from the same numerical response.

This distinction is exposed most clearly by the K2 velocity sequence. The preregistered positive K2 response is far from both tested GDM directions in the same-definition CLASS total-velocity channel, and the separation survives removal of the full scale-independent component at each redshift. Leave-one-scale and leave-one-redshift tests further show that the positive result is not concentrated in a single support node. Taken in isolation, this sequence would suggest a robust dynamic discriminator.

The two-sided control changes that interpretation without invalidating any of the positive-oriented measurements. The fresh negative K2 velocity-shape response is nearly antiparallel to the positive K2 response, with a mutual angle of `179.9078 deg`, yet it lies only `13.5503 deg` and `15.0709 deg` from the two positive GDM axes. Thus the positive and negative finite displacements empirically trace almost the same one-dimensional nuisance line. The large positive-oriented `~165 deg` separation is therefore compatible with a small sign-invariant principal angle.

This point can be seen directly from one-dimensional response geometry. For a nuisance line `span(u)`, the principal angle is `acos(|u dot v|)` rather than `acos(u dot v)`. Applied retrospectively to the Exp071J positive K2 direction, the line-principal angles are `13.5613 deg` and `15.0729 deg`. The prospectively computed negative K2 experiment gives `13.5503 deg` and `15.0709 deg`, differing by only `0.0110 deg` and `0.0020 deg`. The prospective two-sided experiment therefore does more than supply an opposite-sign check: it empirically validates the local nuisance-line interpretation for this operator.

The same geometry provides an important warning for the temporal result. Exp071H legitimately classifies the positive K2 finite-bin temporal response as separated from both positive GDM axes under the preregistered oriented-angle rule, with angles `138.1006 deg` and `137.0973 deg`. But the corresponding line-principal angles are only `41.8994 deg` and `42.9027 deg`. We do not retroactively reclassify Exp071H, because its test object and classification were frozen prospectively. Instead, the line diagnostic identifies the next falsification target: a fresh negative-K2 temporal computation should determine whether the finite displacement shares the same near-antisymmetry seen in velocity space.

The static controls establish a complementary lesson. Adding observables is not equivalent to adding independent identifying information. Weyl and metric-slip responses do contain nonredundant structure: the tested GDM sound-speed and viscosity directions can be almost coincident in one metric response while strongly separated in slip. Nevertheless, the known-sector K2 deformation remains close to the GDM sound-speed-like direction after the frozen Weyl-plus-slip combination and again after adding matter power. The failure therefore cannot be summarized as “not enough channels.” The relevant issue is whether the added channels change the geometry of the nuisance and target subspaces in a way that survives the chosen metric and physical support.

This conclusion is consistent with, but distinct from, established GDM literature. Previous work has emphasized that sound-speed and viscosity effects can be degenerate in restricted cosmological data and that combining observables can improve constraints. DSIR-2 asks a different adversarial question: whether an apparent discriminator remains specific after a known-sector mimic is introduced and after the nuisance object itself is defined in a sign-invariant way. The answer in the present test bank is conditional rather than universal. Some operators strongly rotate an oriented response, but that rotation does not automatically produce mechanism identifiability.

The result also clarifies why DSIR-2 stops short of an observational claim. A theory/provider response vector is upstream of tracer definitions, finite measurement kernels, covariance weighting, nuisance marginalization and likelihood geometry. The ACT x unWISE support failure and the contrasting finite-operator outcomes for BOSS and KiDS show that provider completeness does not imply observational admissibility. Even a genuine principal-angle separation in provider space could disappear, strengthen or become unmeasurable after a survey operator and nuisance quotient are applied.

For this reason, the natural downstream object is not an unweighted response angle but a covariance-aware quotient geometry. Let a measurement operator map theory responses into data space and let the columns of `N` span nuisance tangents. A survey-level specificity test should compare target directions only after projecting or quotienting by the nuisance span, ideally in the metric induced by the data covariance. That construction belongs to DSIR-3 and is not inferred from the present theory-space results.

The broader methodological implication is simple. A response fingerprint should not be promoted from a large angle measured for one chosen signed perturbation. At minimum, specificity should be tested against physically admissible sign changes, finite-step nonlinearity, support deletion and relevant nuisance subspaces. A discriminator that survives those controls is stronger evidence than one that merely enlarges the dimensionality of the response vector. Conversely, failure under a stronger nuisance definition is itself informative because it identifies which part of the apparent mechanism information was coordinate- or orientation-dependent.

## Limitations

The present study examines a deliberately finite mechanism bank and a finite set of response operators. It does not prove that all dark-sector and known-sector mechanisms share the same degeneracies. The K2 construction is a targeted known-sector control, not a complete basis for baryonic, neutrino, primordial-spectrum or calibration uncertainty. The frozen `45 deg` separator is an experiment-specific convention rather than a universal criterion of distinguishability. The response metrics used here are not covariance-whitened, and the theory/provider support is not a substitute for survey likelihood support.

The sign-invariant line analysis is exact only for the one-dimensional span of the measured response direction. A finite negative parameter displacement need not be exactly antiparallel when nonlinear response curvature is appreciable. Exp071L explicitly checks this issue for the velocity-shape operator and finds near-antisymmetry; the corresponding temporal check remains to be run. Higher-dimensional nuisance families require principal angles between subspaces rather than the one-dimensional formula used here.

Finally, the GDM directions used in the current chain are local response axes of the pinned solver setup. The paper does not claim that these axes exhaust all viable dark-sector phenomenology or that separation from them implies separation from arbitrary modified-gravity, interacting-dark-sector or nonlinear models.

## Conclusion draft

DSIR-2 establishes an adversarial hierarchy for response-space specificity. A known-sector fixed-total-matter redistribution falsifies a dark-specific interpretation of the inherited matter-morphology criterion. Static metric augmentation adds genuine information but leaves a sound-speed-like ambiguity. Temporal and velocity operators generate large separations for the preregistered positive K2 displacement, and the positive velocity result survives amplitude and support robustness tests. Yet the prospective negative-K2 velocity experiment restores close overlap and shows that the robust oriented separation is not a sign-invariant property of the physical nuisance line.

The resulting lesson is not that response geometry fails, but that it must be defined at the correct physical level. Channel, operator, metric, support, orientation and nuisance-subspace definition are all part of the inference problem. Treating failed specificity tests as first-class results turns response-space reconstruction from a fingerprint search into a controlled falsification program. The next step is to carry this logic into covariance-whitened, nuisance-quotiented observational space rather than to promote theory-space angles directly to detections.
